# Degree Lens Curriculum — 8 Modules

*From "I can play the scale" to "I can compose over changes." Assumes full CAGED fluency (you have it). Everything in D major / B minor unless noted; everything transposes for free because it's all degrees.*

**Cadence suggestion:** one module ≈ 1–2 weeks at 20–30 min/day. Modules 1–4 are the spine; 5–8 can interleave. Guitar in hand for every drill. No drill requires reading standard notation.

---

## Module 1 — Relabel the map (shapes → degrees)

**Goal:** See degrees, not shapes or note names, in all five CAGED positions.

**Theory (short):** Your CAGED map currently answers "where are the notes of D major?" It must start answering "where is each *degree*?" This is 90% relabeling work, 10% new information.

**Drills**
1. *Position audit.* For each of the five D-major CAGED positions, say the degree of every note as you play it ascending/descending. Slowly. Out loud (naming out loud is what converts recognition into recall).
2. *Degree sniping.* Metronome at 60. Each click, name a random degree (use a dice app or the workbook app's randomizer) and play it in the current position within one beat. All five positions.
3. *Cross-neck degree runs.* Play only degree 3, everywhere on the neck, low to high. Then only 6. Then only 7. (These three matter most later.)

**Done when:** given any position and any degree, you can put a finger on it in under a second, without counting from the root.

---

## Module 2 — The Seven Overlays

**Goal:** For any diatonic chord, instantly light up its degree-trio inside any position.

**Theory:** The table from file 01, §2. Regenerate it from the rules (n, n+2, n+4; 7th = n−1) rather than memorizing rows.

**Drills**
1. *Overlay recitation (no guitar).* Shuffle I–vii°; for each, say the trio in <1 sec. "IV → 4, 6, 1." Do it while walking, driving, waiting for a pipeline to finish.
2. *Arpeggio-in-position.* Pick one CAGED position. Play the I trio as an arpeggio (only those degrees, any octaves available in the position). Then ii, then iii... through vii°. Stay in position the whole time. Repeat in all five positions over a week.
3. *Fresh-note drill.* For each pair of adjacent chords in a progression, name the fresh note (the degree in the new chord that wasn't in the old one). E.g., I→IV: fresh notes 4 and 6. This trains change-announcing.

**Done when:** you can arpeggiate the full diatonic cycle (I ii iii IV V vi vii°) in one position, continuously, at 80 bpm quarter notes, without pausing to think.

---

## Module 3 — Guide tones and the half-step engine

**Goal:** Hear and finger the 3rds-and-7ths skeleton that makes solos sound "inside."

**Theory:** Guide tones = n+2 and n−1. Between chords whose roots move by 4th/5th (the most common motion), one guide tone always resolves by half or whole step while the other holds. Over ii–V–I: ii's guide tones (4, 1) → V's (7, 4): the 1 drops to 7, the 4 holds. Then V→I: 7 rises to 1, 4 falls to 3. Two voices, tiny moves, maximum harmony.

**Drills**
1. *Two-note comping.* Over a looped backing track (or the app's progression stepper), play *only* the two guide tones of each chord, on strings 3–4 or 2–3. Feel which note moves at each change.
2. *Guide-tone melody.* Improvise using only guide tones plus one neighbor note each. Sounds shockingly musical for how few notes it is.
3. *ii–V–I in all five positions,* guide tones only.

**Done when:** over I–vi–ii–V looped, you can play a continuous quarter-note line using ≥70% guide tones without looking at a chart.

## Module 4 — Target-tone soloing (the core skill)

**Goal:** Phrases that land on a chord tone of the *arriving* chord, on the downbeat of the change.

**Theory:** A solo "follows the changes" if the strong beats agree with the harmony. Everything between strong beats can be scale tones, chromatic approaches, anything. Approach devices, in order of spice: (a) diatonic neighbor from above; (b) diatonic neighbor from below; (c) chromatic from below (half step, even if outside the scale); (d) enclosure — above then below then land.

**Drills** (all over the Dsus2–Bm7–G–A loop; the app steps the progression visually)
1. *Whole-note skeleton.* One note per chord, the target: 6 → 4 (or 6) → 7 → resolve 1. Just breathe with the changes.
2. *Approach ramps.* Same targets, but arrive via device (a), then (b), then (c), then (d). One device per lap of the progression.
3. *Half-time phrases.* Two bars of free play (any scale tones), landing on the target at each change. Record yourself; verify landings.
4. *Change the targets.* New lap: land on the *other* guide tone of each chord. Notice the different color.

**Reference phrase** (E-shape zone; from file 01 §5):

```
        Dsus2                Bm7                  G                  A        (→ Dsus2)
e|---------------10--|--7---9--10--9--7--|-------------------|--9~~~~~~~~|--10---
B|--10--12--10-------|-------------------|--8--10--8---------|-----------|-------
G|-------------------|-------------------|------------(7)-9--|-----------|-------
     5   1   5   1       6  7  1  7  6       4   5  4  1  2      7 (hold)     1
```

**Done when:** you can solo two full laps of Dsus2–Bm7–G–A, hitting a correct target on ≥7 of 8 changes, recorded and self-verified.

## Module 5 — Triads on string sets (nine windows per chord)

**Goal:** Own every chord in nine small places, so "where else can I play G" never comes up again.

**Theory:** File 01 §4. Three string sets (e-B-G, B-G-D, G-D-A), three inversions each. Every window is the chord's trio in a different stacking. Learn them as *degree stacks*, not as new shapes: root position = n / n+2 / n+4 low-to-high; 1st inversion starts on n+2; 2nd starts on n+4.

**Worked set — IV chord (4·6·1), top string set, D major:**

```
        1st inv        2nd inv        root pos
e|--3--(4)------|--7--(6)------|--10--(1)-----|
B|--3--(1)------|--8--(4)------|--12--(6)-----|
G|--4--(6)------|--7--(1)------|--12--(4)-----|
```

**Drills**
1. *One chord, nine windows.* Take IV. Play all nine windows ascending the neck, naming the inversion. Repeat for I, V, vi.
2. *Progression in one lane.* Play Dsus2–Bm7–G–A using only the top string set, choosing whichever inversion is nearest (voice leading by laziness — the correct kind).
3. *Triad → lick.* Arpeggiate a window, then decorate it with one scale tone. This is how 80% of melodic country/pop/neo-soul licks are built.

**Done when:** for any diatonic chord, you can find the nearest triad window to wherever your hand currently is, in under two seconds.

## Module 6 — The BB box as home base (minor lens, part 1)

**Goal:** Play full progressions without leaving your favorite box.

**Theory:** The box, relabeled in major-map degrees, contains 2–4 tones of *every* overlay. The constraint (one position) is a feature: with nowhere to run, you're forced to make the changes with note *choice* rather than position changes — which is precisely the skill.

**Drills**
1. *Box census.* Chart your BB box (for B minor, wherever you play it) and label every note with (a) its major-map degree and (b) its minor-lens label. One-time exercise, keep the chart.
2. *Overlay-in-box.* Step through I–vii°; for each chord, play only its available tones inside the box.
3. *Boxed target solo.* Module 4's drill, but confined to the box. The 4 over the G chord and the 7 over the A chord are both in there — find them.
4. *Pentatonic + one.* Solo in B minor pentatonic; each time the IV (G) arrives, add the one non-pentatonic degree (4/♭6-lens) as a landing. Each time V (A) arrives, add 7.

**Done when:** a listener (or you, on recording) can identify the chord changes from your boxed solo alone, backing track muted.

## Module 7 — The minor lens, complete

**Goal:** Think "minor progression," play "major map," with zero translation lag.

**Theory:** Minor tonic = major degree 6. The relabel dictionary (file 01 §3). Common minor progressions pre-translated: i–♭VI–♭VII = vi–IV–V; i–iv–v = vi–ii–iii; the "Zombie" i–♭III–♭VII–♭VI = vi–I–V–IV.

**Drills**
1. *Dictionary sprints.* Hear/say a minor degree ("minor 4"), answer the major-map degree ("2"). Both directions. No guitar needed.
2. *B-minor progression practice.* Loop Bm–G–A (i–♭VI–♭VII), solo using overlays vi / IV / V. Note that this is *literally the same three overlays* as the Module 4 progression minus the sus — you already know it.
3. *The harmonic-minor cameo.* In minor keys, the v is often played as a major V (in B minor: F♯ major, containing A♯). That A♯ is the **one note not on your map** — major-map ♯5. Drill: over Bm–F♯7–Bm, raise the 5 to ♯5 only while the F♯7 sounds, resolve ♯5→6. This is the single most dramatic note in minor-key soloing; it costs you exactly one relabel.

**Done when:** given any i–?–? minor progression named in minor numerals, you can state the major-map overlays instantly and solo over it targeting correctly.

## Module 8 — Composition: from targeting to *saying something*

**Goal:** Write (not just improvise) a 16-bar solo over Dsus2–Bm7–G–A.

**Theory:** Melody = motif + variation. A motif is 3–6 notes with a rhythm. Variations that preserve identity: transpose it diatonically (same shape, start on a different degree — trivially easy in the degree lens: shift every number by the same amount, wrap at 7); invert its contour; keep the rhythm, change the pitches to fit the new overlay; keep the pitches, displace the rhythm. Question/answer phrasing: 2-bar phrase ending *off* the tonic (question), 2-bar phrase ending *on* a stable tone — 1, 3, or 5 of the current chord (answer).

**Drills**
1. *Motif farming.* Improvise over the loop for 5 minutes, recording. Harvest the one 3–6 note fragment you liked most. Write it down as degrees + rhythm.
2. *Diatonic transposition.* Take the motif, shift it to start on each chord's root degree as the progression moves. (Motif 6-7-1 over Bm7 becomes 4-5-6 over G becomes 5-6-7 over A.) Congratulations — this is a *sequence*, the oldest compositional trick in the book, and in the degree lens it's just addition.
3. *Q/A assembly.* Build: bars 1–2 motif (question), bars 3–4 varied motif landing on target (answer). Repeat with variation for bars 5–8. That's half a solo.
4. *Full 16.* Structure: A (8 bars, boxed, sparse) — A′ (8 bars, escape the box upward via a triad window from Module 5, denser, peak on the 7-over-A moment, resolve). Tab it out. Play it until it's *yours*, then let it mutate.

**Done when:** you have one written solo you'd play for another guitarist, and you can explain every landing note in degrees.

## Maintenance loop (post-curriculum, 10 min/day)

1. Random key, random position, one lap of overlay arpeggios (Module 2).
2. One lap of target-tone soloing over a random diatonic progression (Module 4).
3. One motif, one diatonic transposition (Module 8).

Rotate major/minor lens daily. That's the whole gym.
